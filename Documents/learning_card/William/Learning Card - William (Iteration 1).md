# Learning Card

## HYPOTHESIS
We believed that university students often choose courses based mainly on convenience or because they simply follow what is already given to them, rather than intentionally choosing courses based on the skills they want to build. We also believed this happens because students do not clearly understand what skills each course develops or how those skills connect to future career paths. Therefore, we believed that a tool that maps courses to skills and careers would help students make more intentional course decisions.

## OBSERVATION
We observed that this hypothesis was only partly correct. Across the 4 interviews, students were not mostly convenience-driven. Instead, most of them were constrained by fixed course plans, mandatory course structures, limited elective flexibility, or degree requirements. When they did have flexibility, they tended to choose courses based on interest, relevance to their major, or future career goals, not mainly because the course was easy or convenient.

At the same time, we did observe a real gap: students often understood the value of individual courses, but they struggled more with understanding the bigger picture of how courses connect to each other, what order makes sense, and how their degree builds toward a career. In the cybersecurity interview, for example, the student described struggling because the program structure did not make prerequisite flow clear, and later said it was hard to connect all their courses into one coherent skill set.

## LEARNING INSIGHTS
From that, we learnt that the main problem is not simply that students are lazy or convenience-first. The deeper issue is that students often lack visibility into:

1. how courses build on one another  
2. what concrete skills each course contributes  
3. how those skills relate to future jobs  

We also learnt that students can usually name a skill from a recent course, but they are less confident when asked to explain their overall skill development across the degree. So the opportunity is less about “stopping bad choices” and more about helping students see a clearer pathway between courses, skills, and careers.

## THEREFORE WE WILL
Therefore we will refine our idea and focus less on solving “convenience-based course selection” and more on solving the problem of unclear academic and career pathway visibility.

We will design **SkillPath** as a tool that helps students:

- see how courses connect across semesters  
- understand the concrete skills each course develops  
- identify how those skills align with possible careers  
- make better elective decisions when flexibility exists  

This means our next step should be to test whether students would find value in features such as course-to-skill mapping, career-path recommendations, and prerequisite / progression visualisation rather than only assuming they need help avoiding convenience-based choices.
