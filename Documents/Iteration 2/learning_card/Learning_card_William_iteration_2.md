# Learning Card

## Hypothesis

We believe that university students often select courses based on convenience factors (e.g., timetable fit, perceived difficulty, recommendations from friends) rather than focusing on the specific skills they aim to develop. We propose that a tool that shows how courses connect to skills and career paths will help students make more intentional, strategic course selections.

## Observations

Based on the 5 interviews I have conducted, I observed that all 5 interviewees (100%) expressed interest in using a tool that maps courses to skills and career paths, all 5 interviewees (100%) agreed the tool would help them make more intentional course selections, and all 5 interviewees (100%) said the tool would help them clearly understand the skills developed by each course. All three success criteria (70%, 60%, 50%) in the test card were exceeded based on my interviews. Students described their current course selection process as opaque, slow, and convenience-driven — relying on friend recommendations, perceived difficulty, parental influence, or brief course descriptions that did not specify the skills gained. $\frac{2}{5}$ interviewees expressed a lack of confidence that their current courses were leading them to their desired career. All 5 said they would use the tool every time they need to choose a course, with one qualifying that the value would have been higher earlier in their degree.

## Learning Insights

The core hypothesis is supported based on my interviews. Students lack skill-level and career-path visibility when choosing courses. I also learnt that:

- Course descriptions explain content but not skills. Multiple interviewees explicitly distinguished between "what the course is about" (provided) and "what skills you walk away with" (missing). The tool's primary value is centralising information that is currently scattered or absent — not adding a new information source.
- Skill articulation is a co-equal use case to course selection. Interviewees struggled to list the skills they had already gained from past courses, defaulting to vague, general capabilities. A tool that helps students name what they have learned would also support CV writing, internship applications, and self-reflection.
- Adoption intent depends on degree stage. One interviewee qualified their answer with "if it was earlier in my degree" — late-stage students see less value because they have already chosen most of their courses. Targeting early- and mid-degree students will yield the strongest adoption.
- Concrete feature requests surfaced beyond the original scope, including side-by-side course comparison, semester availability and prerequisite eligibility, visibility of past student experiences, skill-gap analysis (what a course does not teach), and a simple, low-friction UI.
- Students do not naturally frame courses in terms of skills. Interviewees described thinking about courses in terms of content, difficulty, peer recommendations, or timetable fit, but rarely in terms of the specific skills developed.

## Future Action

Therefore, I will accept the hypothesis that students need skill-level and career-path course information and proceed to design and prototype the tool. In the next iteration, we will investigate:

- How to surface specific, concrete skill breakdowns per course rather than restating the brief descriptions students already find on existing course profiles.
- How to design the tool so that career-path mapping builds on top of a clear skills-per-course view, rather than positioning career paths as the entry point.
- Whether a "skills I've already gained" view that helps students articulate past learning for CVs and portfolios increases engagement and perceived value.
- How to incorporate the highest-signal feature requests — side-by-side course comparison, semester availability, and prerequisite eligibility — into a simple, low-friction interface.
