# Learning Card

## Hypothesis

We believe that university students often select courses based on convenience factors (e.g., timetable fit, perceived difficulty, recommendations from friends) rather than focusing on the specific skills they aim to develop. We propose that a tool that shows how courses connect to skills and career paths will help students make more intentional, strategic course selections.

## Observations

Based on the 3 interviews I have conducted, I observed that all 3 interviewees (100%) expressed interest in using a tool that maps courses to skills and career paths, all 3 interviewees (100%) agreed the tool would help them making more intentional course selections, and all 3 interviewees (100%) found it is difficult to articulate skills gained from their courses and said a tool aiding this will be helpful and valuable. All three success criteria (70%, 60%, 50%) in test card are all exceeded based on my interview. Students described their current course selection process as uninformed, random, guessing, relying on friend recommendations, course requirements, or interest alone. $\frac{2}{3}$ interviewees expressed the lack of confident that their current courses were leading them to their desired career. All 3 said they would use the tool every time they need to choose a course and would recommend it to their peers.

## Learning Insights

The core hypothesis is supported based on my interview. Students lack skill-level and career-path visibility when choosing courses. I also learnt that:

- Specificity of skill descriptions is critical. One interviewee explicitly noted that vague label like "hands-on skills" or "computer skills" are not useful. Interviewee expect the tool to have surface specific, concrete skill breakdowns and examples.
- UX quality and accuracy are a prerequisite for SkillPath. One interviewee specifically requested the tool to be simple, not overwhelming, with clear visuals and a non-generic feel.
- Students do not naturally frame courses in terms of skills. All three interviewees described thinking about courses in terms of content, grades, requirements, or peer recommendations, but not skills.

## Future Action

Therefore, I will accept the hypothesis that students need skill-level and career-path course information and proceed to design and prototype SkillPath. In the next iteration, we will investigate:

- How to surface specific, concrete skill breakdowns per course rather than generic skill categories.
- How to design a simple, visual, and prevent overwhelming interface that students can trust and navigate quickly.
- Whether a skill tracker that helps students articulate gained skills for resumes and internship applications increases engagement and perceived value.
