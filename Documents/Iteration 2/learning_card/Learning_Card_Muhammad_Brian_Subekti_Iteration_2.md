# Learning Card

## Hypothesis

We believe that university students often select courses based on convenience factors (e.g., timetable fit, perceived difficulty, recommendations from friends) rather than focusing on the specific skills they aim to develop. We propose that a tool that shows how courses connect to skills and career paths will help students make more intentional, strategic course selections.

## Observations

Based on the 5 interviews I have conducted, I observed that all 5 interviewees (100%) expressed interest in using a tool that maps courses to skills and career paths, all 5 interviewees (100%) agreed the tool would help them make more intentional course selections, and $\frac{4}{5}$ interviewees (80%) found it difficult to articulate skills gained from their courses and said a tool aiding this would be helpful and valuable. All three success criteria (70%, 60%, 50%) in the test card were exceeded based on my interviews. Students described their current course selection process as relying on friend recommendations, perceived difficulty, parental influence, or course descriptions that they felt were too abstract. $\frac{2}{5}$ interviewees expressed a lack of confidence that their current courses were leading them to their desired career, while another openly admitted having no career direction at all. All 5 said they would use the tool every time they need to choose a course, and all 5 said they would recommend it to their peers (one with the caveat that they would only recommend it after using it themselves).

## Learning Insights

The core hypothesis is supported based on my interviews. Students lack skill-level and career-path visibility when choosing courses. I also learnt that:

- Course descriptions create the illusion of skill information without actually providing it. One interviewee explicitly described the existing skill bullet points on course profiles as "very abstract" and "not giving you the actual skills." The tool must surface specific, concrete skill breakdowns rather than restating what course profiles already say.
- Skill articulation is a co-equal use case to course selection. Three interviewees independently raised CV-building without prompting, with one noting that "sometimes we just don't have the word for it." A third-year interviewee said they could not realistically list the skills gained from their ~8 completed courses.
- The tool serves two distinct user modes. Some interviewees wanted *exploration* (no career goal, or wanting to switch direction) while others wanted *validation* (already confident, seeking reassurance). The product flow should accommodate both rather than assuming a single user journey.
- Trust must be earned, not assumed. One interviewee said they would still do their own research alongside the tool because "I don't want to look at only one platform." Another asked whether existing solutions already cover this need. Adoption depends on demonstrable quality and a clear differentiator.
- Students do not naturally frame courses in terms of skills. Interviewees described thinking about courses in terms of content, regret, parental expectation, or peer recommendations — rarely in terms of the specific skills developed.

## Future Action

Therefore, I will accept the hypothesis that students need skill-level and career-path course information and proceed to design and prototype SkillPath. In the next iteration, we will investigate:

- How to surface specific, concrete skill breakdowns per course rather than the abstract bullet points already present in existing course profiles.
- How to design the tool so it accommodates both exploration (students without a career goal) and validation (students seeking reassurance) within a single, simple interface.
- Whether a skill tracker that helps students articulate gained skills for CVs and internship applications increases engagement and perceived value.
- Whether comparable existing solutions (e.g., LinkedIn skill mapping) already address this need, and how SkillPath can clearly differentiate itself.
