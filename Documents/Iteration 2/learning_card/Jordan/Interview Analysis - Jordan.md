## 4. What Students Need

1. **Clear Visual Mapping (Gamification):** Students respond highly to the idea of a "skill tree" or a clear visual roadmap that shows progression, rather than just lists of text.
2. **Help Articulating Skills:** Students need help putting their learned concepts into professional words for resumes and interviews. They know they learned *something*, but lack the vocabulary to express it.
3. **Direct Course Comparisons:** A feature that highlights the nuanced differences between courses that sound identical on paper (e.g., two different programming courses).
4. **Social Proof & Ratings:** For a tool to be adopted, some students need to see that it is trusted, highly rated, and approved by their peers.
5. **Nuanced Skill Extraction:** Acknowledgment that one course can yield different skills depending on what the student focuses on (e.g., backend vs. frontend in a database course).

## 6. Unexpected Insights That Challenge Assumptions

1. **Gamification drives motivation:** Interviewee #2 explicitly linked the concept of course mapping to video game "skill trees." The appeal isn't just data organization; it's the psychological reward of unlocking progression paths.
2. **Articulation is the hurdle, not just memory:** Interviewee #4 noted they aren't necessarily forgetting what they learned, but they struggle to "put it into words." The tool needs to act as a translator between academic work and professional vocabulary.
3. **Trust requires social proof:** Interviewee #5 wouldn't automatically use the tool just because it exists; they explicitly stated it needs "good ratings" and approval from others. Data alone isn't enough to drive adoption without community trust.
4. **Skills are subjective to the user's focus:** Interviewee #1 pointed out a flaw in standard mapping: a database course gives UI/Frontend skills to one student and Backend skills to another, depending on their interest. A static 1-to-1 course-to-skill map might be too rigid.
